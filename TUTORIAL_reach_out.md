# tutorial: reach out from a job (draft, 2 Oct 2026)

For the "Reach out from a job" panel. Goal: get a referral or a first conversation at a company you applied to. Written for international students in the Netherlands. Status: draft for the owner to approve. Nothing here is built in the app yet.

## what to claim (read this before writing anything shown to users)

- Sourced: a referred application passed the first screen in 52% of cases against 35% for all applications, among 54 million applications (Ashby 2026). That is x1.49. It is already in the app (`FACTORS.referral`, `what-improves-callbacks.ts`).
- Not sourced as causal: Ashby calls it an aggregate. Referred people may simply be stronger. Say "referred applications pass the first screen more often", never "a referral makes you x times more likely to get hired".
- Do NOT use: "4x", "10x", "40% of hires", "70% of Dutch jobs are never posted". They come from recruiting blogs and vendors, they disagree with each other (2%/11%, 6%/37%, 7%/40%) and no study of international students in the Netherlands backs them. The honest line: "no study measures this for international graduates in the Netherlands".
- "Massively" is not supported. The sourced effect is real but moderate. The bigger win is practical: a person inside reads your application and tells you what the team wants.

## stronger claims you can pick from (all with sources, owner decides what to show)

Checked at the source:
- 1 in 16 referred candidates were hired, against 1 in 152 for people who applied directly (1 in 72 sourced by recruiters, 1 in 22 agency). Lever, over 4 million candidates, about 1,000 employers, Aug 2015 to Jul 2016, US vendor data, and referrals were 16% of hires. About 9.5x better than a direct application. https://www.shrm.org/topics-tools/news/talent-acquisition/lever-study-shows-1-100-candidates-hired
- 52% vs 35% first-screen pass, x1.49. Ashby 2026, 54 million applications. In the app already.

Reported by recruiting sites, NOT checked at the primary source (the three sets disagree, so show a range and name the source, or leave out):
- "about 7% of applicants but about 40% of hires": theundercoverrecruiter.com
- "2% of applicants, 11% of hires" (about 10x): one of the vendor reports in the search results
- "6% of applications, 37% of hires": another vendor report
- "up to 70% of Dutch roles are filled before they are posted": expatnetherlandshub.com guide, no data behind it. Weakest claim here.

Honest way to say it if you want it strong: "in one large study of hiring data, referred candidates were hired about 9 times more often than people who applied directly. It is vendor data from the US and says nothing about international students in the Netherlands."

## why this fits international students in the Netherlands

1. Dutch workplaces are informal and direct. First names, no titles, short messages that say what you want. Long flattering messages read as odd. (Qualitative: expat and recruiter guides, listed below.)
2. If you finished a degree in the last 3 years, the orientation year (zoekjaar) lets you work without a separate work permit (government.nl). So the person you write to can honestly be told "I can start without a work permit". This removes the usual fear that hiring a foreigner is hard. Check your own status first. The permit has an application fee and the salary lines in the app are from the IND page, not this guide.
3. You usually have no Dutch network, so the people you can reach are the ones a click away: alumni of your own university, people from your country now at the company, and people one step away through a shared connection.

## the four steps (what the user sees)

### 1. keep the job, then find the right people
Press the bookmark on a job you want. We list people at that company in the same kind of role (directors and heads are left out). Pick people in the team you applied to, or next to it (same function, a neighbouring department). Best first: someone who did your degree or a similar one, someone from your country, someone who started in the last 3 years. They remember being new.

### 2. look for a link before you write
Open their profile and check, in this order:
- same university or programme (alumni answer more often)
- a shared connection who can say hi first
- something they posted or built that you can mention in one line
A warm line ("we both studied at X") beats a cold one. Mutual connections are the first thing to look for, so we show them first.

### 3. send a short message, ask for 15 minutes, not for a job
Ask for a coffee chat or a call about the team. Do not ask for a referral in the first message. Offer a specific time window and make it easy to say no.

Connection note (LinkedIn caps the note at 200 characters on free accounts per third-party sources, check on your screen):
> hi [first name], i am a [programme] student at [university], applying to [team] at [company]. could i ask you two questions about the team? 15 minutes, any time that suits you.

After they accept, the coffee-chat message:
> thanks for connecting. i applied for [job title] ([link]). i would value 15 minutes on what the team works on and what you look for in someone starting out. i can do a call or coffee in [city], whatever is easier for you. i finished my [degree] in [month year], so i can work here without a work permit through the orientation year.

Delete the last sentence if it does not apply to you.

### 4. ask for the referral only after the chat
In the chat: ask what the team needs, what the first months look like, what made a recent hire stand out. At the end, one clear ask:
> i have applied for [job title]. if you think i am a fit, would you be willing to refer me, or tell me who to send my CV to? i can send you a two-line summary to forward.

If they say no or say nothing, thank them once and move on. One polite follow-up after a week is fine. Then stop.

Press Add to put them in your list, linked to the job, so the app counts the referral in your odds.

## rules for the writing in the app
- lowercase tone, no em dashes, short sentences, no paragraphs of explanation (put the detail behind a "?").
- no number without a source shown next to it.
